**A software video storyboard is a sequence of planned scenes that shows what the audience will see and understand at each moment.** It sits between the script and animation. Its job is to catch unclear explanations while they are still easy to change.

## Begin with the script's job

Each scene should answer a small question: What is the viewer learning here? Which line of the script does the image support? What should be remembered before the next scene begins?

If the narration and visual make separate claims, the viewer has to work too hard. Rewrite the line or change the scene until they support the same idea.

## Show the product with enough context

A storyboard should identify the interface state, the action, and the result. It does not need pixel-perfect UI in every frame, but it should show where attention goes. A cursor move, highlight or simplified screen can make a complex workflow understandable without shrinking the entire dashboard into one shot.

Ask whether someone unfamiliar with the product can follow the action. If they cannot, more animation is unlikely to solve the underlying explanation.

## Mark timing and transitions

Write the approximate duration of each beat and note the transition between scenes. A strong line may need a quiet pause; a workflow may need more time on screen than the script initially suggests. This is also the point to decide where sound or voice should carry meaning, and where a visual can speak for itself.

## Review before producing finished frames

The most valuable feedback at storyboard stage is about meaning: Is the claim accurate? Is a key step missing? Does the sequence match the buyer’s experience? Brand colours and polish matter, but they should not hide a confusing story.

In the [Mina AI process example](/process), the storyboard, voice options and final cuts show how these decisions connect. The [Mina AI case study](/cases) shows the broader brand and film work around the launch.

## What approval should mean

Storyboard approval should confirm the narrative, product actions and scene order. Visual details can still develop, but major changes to the story become more costly once production begins. Keeping the storyboard clear gives both the client and production team a shared reference for the finished film.
